    The Great Leak

    Something is broken. And it isn't you.

    The best analogy for inflation

    Your savings are an ice cube in the sun.

    You can't see it melting day to day, but it is. Inflation doesn't take your money. It makes every unit of it buy less, quietly, every year.

    A thought experiment

    Imagine playing Monopoly…

    …where one player is also the banker,

    and is allowed to print new money whenever they like.

    They never take a single bill from your pile. They just keep adding to theirs, and every bill you hold buys a little less property than before.

    Who wins that game? That's the game we're all playing.

    The Cantillon Effect

    New money isn't shared equally. It flows downhill.

    ⎙Money printerCentral banknew money
    🏛Banks & governmentSpend it firstold prices
    ◈Asset ownersStocks, propertyprices rising
    ⚙BusinessesRaise pricesprices up
    ☺YouWages adjust lastnew prices

    Whoever is closest to the money printer spends the new money while prices are still low. By the time it reaches workers and savers, prices have already gone up.

    The economist Richard Cantillon described this in the 1730s. The result is a hidden transfer: from savers to borrowers, from wage earners to asset owners, from the poor to the rich.

    August 15, 1971

    The day money lost its anchor.

    President Nixon "temporarily" suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold. That temporary measure is still in force today. Since then, money has been backed by nothing but promises, and the numbers started to drift apart.

    The treadmill

    When saving stopped working, everyone was forced to speculate.

    Your grandparents could put money in a bank and expect it to hold. You have to become a part-time fund manager just to stand still: like running on a treadmill that speeds up every year.

    Houses became savings accounts

    People buy homes to protect wealth, not just to live in them. That extra demand prices young families out of the very thing they need most.

    Stocks became inflation shelters

    Part of the "growth" in stock prices is simply the measuring stick shrinking. Economists call this extra price the monetary premium.

    Those without assets fall behind

    If you rent and save in cash, the system works against you. If you already own assets, it works for you. The gap widens every year.

    The bank

    Your money isn't in the bank.

    When you deposit $1,000, the bank lends most of it out. What you hold is an IOU, a promise that the money will be there if not too many people ask at the same time.

    II

    Chapter two

    What Money Really Is

    Before we can fix money, we need to understand what it's for.

    Money = stored time

    Money is a battery for your life's energy.

    You trade hours of your life for money. Money stores those hours so you can use them later: next week, or in thirty years when you retire.

    Good money is a battery that holds its charge. Today's money is a battery that leaks, by design. Every leaked percent is a piece of your life you'll never get back.

    40 hours of your life…slowly draining

    The official plan

    Central banks aim for 2% inflation.

    −50%

    That sounds small. But at 2% a year, money loses half its value in about 35 years, roughly one working lifetime. That isn't a bug. It's the stated goal.

    What makes money good?

    Money is a tool. Some tools are simply better.

    Scarce

    Hard to make more of. Otherwise someone will, and dilute you.

    Durable

    Doesn't rot, rust or break over time.

    Portable

    Easy to carry and send, across a street or across an ocean.

    Divisible

    Can be split into small units for any size of payment.

    Verifiable

    Easy to check that it's real, without trusting anyone.

    Fungible

    Every unit is interchangeable with every other.

    Censorship-resistant

    Nobody can stop you from saving or spending it. In the digital age, this one matters more than ever.

    The most important property is scarcity, because money that's easy to create gets created, and the people holding it pay the price.

    A warning from history

    The tragedy of the glass beads.

    For centuries, glass beads served as money in parts of West Africa. They were hard to make locally, so they held value.

    Then European traders arrived with ships full of beads made cheaply in glass factories. They used them to buy gold, ivory, land, and eventually human beings, paying with money that cost them almost nothing to produce.

    Whoever can cheaply create your money can buy your life's work for nothing.

    Today, the glass beads are printed by central banks.

    Why gold won, and then lost

    Gold was the hardest money for 5,000 years.

    All the gold ever mined, about 216,000 tonnes, equals roughly 60 years of current production. Even when gold's price soars, miners can only add about 1.5–2% per year. Hard to make, so it holds value.

    But gold has weaknesses: it's heavy, expensive to store, and hard to verify. So people handed it to banks in exchange for paper claims, and the paper slowly replaced the gold.

    1. 1933US Executive Order 6102 makes it illegal for citizens to hold gold. They must sell it to the government at $20.67 an ounce. Months later the government reprices gold to $35, a 41% devaluation of the dollar.
    2. 1944Bretton Woods: the world's currencies are pegged to the dollar, and the dollar to gold.
    3. 1971The last link between money and gold is cut. From then on, every currency on Earth is backed by promises alone.
    4. 2009Something new appears.
    III

    Chapter three

    Enter Bitcoin

    Money with rules that no one can change.

    January 3, 2009

    A message hidden in the very first block.

    In the middle of the financial crisis, an anonymous creator named Satoshi Nakamoto launched Bitcoin. Embedded in its first block was a newspaper headline about bank bailouts: a timestamp, and a statement of purpose.

    Satoshi stepped away in 2011 and has never returned. No CEO. No company. No headquarters. No one to arrest, bribe, or pressure.

    Every 2,016 blocks (about two weeks), Bitcoin automatically adjusts the difficulty of mining so that a new block appears about every 10 minutes, no matter how many machines join. For the first time in history, the supply of money is completely immune to demand.

    How it works, without the jargon

    Think of a public notebook that nobody owns.

    1

    A shared notebook

    Every bitcoin transaction ever made is written in one ledger. Identical copies are kept by tens of thousands of computers ("nodes") around the world. Anyone can keep a copy.

    2

    A lottery for the next page

    Every ~10 minutes, "miners" compete to add the next page (a block) by solving a puzzle that is very hard to solve but instant to check. The winner earns new bitcoin plus fees.

    3

    Rules everyone checks

    Every node independently checks every page against the rules. Try to spend coins twice, or create coins from nothing, and the network simply ignores you. No permission needed, and no exceptions.

    Mining turns real-world energy into security, much like digging gold out of the ground. The cost can't be faked, so the record can't be cheaply rewritten.

    Try it yourself

    Be a miner for ten seconds.

    Miners repeatedly scramble block data with a "nonce" (a number they keep changing) until the resulting fingerprint (a SHA-256 hash) starts with enough zeros. There's no shortcut: just guessing, millions of times.

    Hard to find, instant to verify. Change a single letter and the fingerprint changes completely. That's why history can't be quietly edited.

    Press start. Your browser will guess until it finds a valid hash.

    Simplified: real Bitcoin double-hashes an 80-byte block header, and the network as a whole makes about a sextillion guesses per second.

    Digital scarcity

    The first digital thing that can't be copied.

    Photos, songs, files: anything digital can be copied for free, endlessly. That's why digital money always needed a bank in the middle, to stop you spending the same dollar twice.

    Bitcoin solved this "double-spend problem" without any middleman. That's the invention. What email did for letters, bitcoin does for value: it moves across the planet in minutes, at any hour, with nobody's approval.

    You don't need a whole one

    1 BTC = 100,000,000 sats

    Bitcoin is divisible into "satoshis", like a dollar into cents, but with far more room. You can start with a few dollars' worth.

    True Ownership

    Security, self-custody, and money that can't be stopped.

    Self-custody

    Your entire bank, in 12 words.

    A bitcoin wallet is controlled by a seed phrase: 12 or 24 ordinary words. Whoever knows the words controls the bitcoin. Nobody else, ever.

    Write them on paper, stamp them into steel, or memorize them, and you can carry your life savings across any border in your head. No asset in history has ever been this portable.

      Not your keys, not your coins.

It's like trying to secretly edit a book that tens of thousands of strangers hold identical copies of, while it gains a new page every ten minutes.

      Don't trust. Verify.

      Separation of money and state.

      • Rules are enforced by users, not rulers
      • Changing them requires near-universal agreement

      VI

      Chapter six

      The Sovereign Mindset

      Fix the money, fix the world.

      Time preference

      When money holds its value, you can think in decades.

      Melting money teaches

      • Spend it now, it's worth less tomorrow
      • Borrow, because debt gets inflated away
      • Chase returns just to stand still
      • Short-term thinking, everywhere
      →

      Sound money teaches

      • Saving is rewarded, not punished
      • Buy fewer, better things that last
      • Invest only in what's truly worth it
      • Plan for your children's children

      Economists call this time preference: how much you value the future compared to the present. People with a low time preference planted orchards and built cathedrals they would never see finished. Good money makes patience rational again.

      The deflation myth

      Prices are supposed to fall.

      Technology makes nearly everything cheaper to produce: computing, TVs, solar panels, communication. In a world of honest money, those gains reach you as lower prices.

      In ours, they're inflated away by new money before you ever see them. Falling prices from progress aren't a crisis. They're humanity's productivity, finally shared with everyone.

      "But nobody will spend!"

      TVs and phones get cheaper and better every single year, and people still buy them. You buy what you need when you need it. You just stop buying junk you don't.

      Further reading

      Jeff Booth's The Price of Tomorrow explains why technology is deflationary, and why our money system fights it.

      Homes for living

      Let homes be homes again.

      When money melts, people store their savings in property. That extra demand pushes prices beyond what young families can pay, so a generation rents while homes sit as savings accounts.

      Bitcoin gives savings somewhere else to live: scarce, portable, and it doesn't need a roof. Every saver who chooses it is one less bidder against a first-time buyer.

      Lifeboats as lockers

      Using homes to store wealth is like using lifeboats as storage lockers. It works for whoever owns them, until someone needs a seat.

      3.5 → 5.2Years of median household income needed to buy a median new home, 1984 vs 2024US Census Bureau

      Energy

      Mining runs on energy nobody else wants.

      Bitcoin miners can plug in anywhere and switch off in seconds. That makes them a unique customer: they buy power that would otherwise be wasted, and step aside when people need it.

      Flared gas

      Oil fields burn off methane, a powerful greenhouse gas. Miners turn it into electricity instead, cutting emissions and paying for it.

      Stranded wind and sun

      New wind and solar farms often produce more power than the grid can take. Miners buy the surplus, helping new projects pay for themselves (Cornell, 2023).

      A flexible grid

      When a heatwave or a storm hits, miners power down within seconds and hand the electricity back to homes and hospitals.

      Think of a sponge: it soaks up energy that would spill and go to waste, and lets go the moment people need it. 52.4% of mining already runs on sustainable energy (Cambridge, 2025).

      Neutral money

      No CEO. No ads. No favorites.

      Bitcoin has no company behind it. It doesn't sell your data, show you ads, take a cut of your payments, or bail anyone out. Big tech, big banks and the largest corporations play by exactly the same rules as a student with a phone.

      Nobody's quarterly profit

      Holding bitcoin doesn't fund a tech giant, a bank's balance sheet or a shareholder dividend. It isn't a share in anyone's company.

      Same rules for everyone

      A billionaire can't print more, buy a better version, or change the rules for themselves.

      Like a language

      Like maths or a language, nobody owns it and everybody can use it. That's what makes it fair.

      A peaceful revolution

      Money that can't be printed for war.

      For centuries, wars have been paid for by printing or debasing money, from Roman coins diluted with cheap metal to the paper money of the 20th century. Nobody votes for that tax. It arrives quietly, as higher prices.

      With money that can't be printed, governments have to pay for everything openly, with taxes people can see and debate. Hard money keeps power accountable.

      The kitchen-table rule

      A family that can only spend what it earns has to talk through every big decision. Sound money brings nations back to the kitchen table.

      No barricades needed

      Bitcoin changes the system without protests, violence or permission. People simply choose better money, one person at a time.

      People first

      For you, your family and your business.

      Hold them yourself, open 24/7, with no permission, no bank hours and no minimum balance.

      Your family

      Save for your children in money that holds its value, and plan an inheritance that no frozen account or middleman can block.

      Your business

      Paid from anywhere

      Accept payments from around the world in minutes, with no chargebacks and no payment processor that can cut you off. Keep reserves that inflation can't eat.

      VII

      Chapter seven

      Honest Questions

      Skepticism is healthy. Here are straight answers.

      The common objections

      “Yes, but…”

      Isn't it way too volatile?

      Yes, today it is. A new monetary asset grows from zero to global scale, and the price swings along the way. Volatility is the price of being early, and it has tended to shrink as adoption grows. As of 2025, every four-year period in bitcoin's history ended higher than it started, but past performance guarantees nothing. Only hold an amount you could leave untouched for four years or more.

      Isn't it used by criminals?

      Mostly not. Chainalysis estimates illicit activity at under 1% of crypto transaction volume, while the UN estimates 2–5% of global GDP is laundered every year, mostly in dollars and euros. And a public ledger that records every transaction forever is a terrible place to hide crime.

      Doesn't it waste energy?

      Energy is what makes the ledger impossible to fake. Whether that's "waste" depends on whether you think honest money is worth securing. Over half of mining now runs on sustainable sources (Cambridge, 2025). Miners can set up anywhere, so they buy stranded and surplus energy that would otherwise be wasted, help fund new wind and solar projects (Cornell, 2023), and help stabilise power grids.

      Can't governments just ban it?

      China banned mining in 2021, and the network's computing power recovered to new highs within about a year. Bitcoin is just information: you can't ban math. Meanwhile, the US approved spot bitcoin ETFs in 2024 and created a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve in 2025.

      Can't someone make a better one?

      You can copy the code, not the network. Thousands have tried. Almost all have a founder, a company, insiders who got coins first, or a team that can change the rules. Bitcoin's fair launch, vanished creator, 17-year track record, and unmatched security can't be copied, just as copying Wikipedia's code wouldn't give you Wikipedia.

      Haven't I already missed it?

      People said that at $1, $100, $1,000, and $10,000. It's been declared "dead" over 400 times. Bitcoin's total value is still a fraction of gold's, and only a small share of humanity holds it in self-custody. Asking this today is like asking whether you'd missed the internet in 1999.

      Isn't it a Ponzi scheme?

      A Ponzi needs a promoter promising returns and paying earlier investors with newer investors' money. Bitcoin has no promoter, no issuer, no promised return, and no one paying anyone. It's open-source software, and every coin is auditable by anyone.

      What if I lose my keys?

      Then nobody can recover them for you. That's the price of true ownership. Start small, practice restoring a wallet from its seed phrase, keep backups in separate safe places, and plan how your family would inherit. Multi-signature setups remove any single point of failure.

      Won't quantum computers break it?

      Today's quantum computers are far from a threat. When they become one, banks, governments and the entire internet face the same challenge. Bitcoin can upgrade too: quantum-resistant signature proposals are already being developed in the open.

      Isn't it too complicated for me?

      You don't need to understand how email servers work to send an email. Modern wallets are as simple as a banking app. Start with a few dollars, practice sending and receiving, and learn the deeper parts over time. The hardest part isn't the technology. It's unlearning what we were taught about money.

      VIII

      Chapter eight

      Your First Steps

      Curiosity first. Sats second. Keys always.

      A simple path

      Study, start small, hold your own keys.

      1

      Learn first

      Spend hours, not minutes. Read, listen, ask questions. Understanding is what lets you hold through the volatility.

      2

      Start small

      Buy a few dollars' worth of sats from a reputable, regulated service. Many people buy a small fixed amount regularly instead of guessing the right moment.

      3

      Take custody

      Withdraw to a wallet you control. For larger amounts, use a hardware wallet that keeps your keys offline.

      4

      Guard the seed

      Write it on paper or steel. Never photograph it, store it in the cloud, or type it into a website. Nobody legitimate will ever ask for it.

      5

      Verify

      When you're ready, run your own node. Then you don't need to trust anyone's word about anything.

      6

      Pass it on

      Explain it to someone you care about. That's how every orange pill has ever spread.

      This presentation is education, not financial advice. Do your own research, and never invest more than you can afford to lose.
